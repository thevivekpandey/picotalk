A. What is Art?
The term 'art' has been debated and defined across various disciplines; however, for the purpose of this unit, we adopt Arthur Danto's notion of art as something that embodies a certain kind of intentionality and meaning (Danto, 1964). In other words, art is not merely about aesthetic qualities but also involves an artist's intention to communicate ideas, emotions, or messages through their creations. This definition allows us to approach artworks as sites where complex socio-political dynamics unfold.

B. Queerness and LGBTQ+ Identities
Queerness refers to sexual orientations and gender identities beyond heterosexual normativity and cisgender experience. It encompasses diverse identities under the umbrella term "LGBTQ+," which stands for lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, questioning/queer, and additional identities such as pansexual, asexual, non-binary, genderfluid, etc. While acknowledging the diversity within the LGBTQ+ community, this unit emphasizes shared experiences of marginalization, stigmatization, and resistance that often inform queer artists' work.

C. Performance Art and Drag Culture
Performance art is a form of visual art where the artist uses their body, voice, and actions to convey meanings and provoke responses from audiences. Often political and subversive, performance art challenges traditional boundaries between mediums like painting, sculpture, theater, dance, and video while engaging viewers in direct ways.

Drag culture, rooted in ballroom scenes, cabaret performances, and crossdressing traditions, revolves around individuals assuming exaggerated gendered personas for entertainment purposes. Although frequently conflated with campy humor or commercialized spectacles like RuPaul's Drag Race, drag performances have long served as platforms for critiquing societal expectations surrounding gender roles, sexuality, and self-expression.

Section 1: Understanding Chocolate and its Composition

Before diving into the process of tempering, let us first understand what chocolate is made up of and why proper handling is crucial. Chocolate primarily consists of cocoa solids, cocoa butter (vegetable fat), sugar, lecithin (an emulsifier), and often milk powder or condensed milk in milk chocolates. When heated or cooled improperly, the cocoa butter crystals within chocolate can form different types of bonds leading to inconsistent texture and appearance. Therefore, learning to control these crystal structures through tempering ensures optimal results.

Section 2: What is Chocolate Tempering?

Tempering is a meticulous process that involves carefully heating and cooling chocolate while continuously stirring it to promote the formation of stable beta crystals in the cocoa butter. These uniform crystals give rise to desirable qualities like glossiness, resistance to melting at room temperature, and a satisfying snap when broken. In other words, well-tempered chocolate becomes easier to work with and presents itself beautifully.

Section 3: Importance of Temperature Control in Tempering

Maintaining precise temperatures during the tempering process is vital. Different methods require varying temperature ranges; however, all involve bringing the chocolate close to its melting point (around 86°F to 90°F for dark chocolate) and then gradually lowering the temperature while constantly agitating the mixture. By controlling the thermal history of the chocolate, confectioners ensure consistent crystal formation resulting in high-quality end products.

Section 4: Common Methods of Chocolate Tempering

There are several ways to temper chocolate, including seeding, tabling, and using a marble slab. Each method caters to specific needs and preferences, requiring unique equipment and techniques. Regardless of which approach you choose, mastering temperature control remains paramount.

A). Seeding Method: This simple yet effective technique entails adding small amounts of already tempered chocolate (known as seeds) to melted chocolate until reaching the desired consistency. As the added chocolate cools down the molten mass, it encourages the growth of stable crystals throughout the batch.

B). Tabling Method: Also known as "working" or "hand-tempering," this labor-intensive process requires spreading thin layers of chocolate onto a smooth surface (like granite or marble) and manually manipulating them by lifting, folding, and pressing repeatedly. Through physical movement and controlled cooling, the chocolate achieves ideal temper.

C). Marble Slab Method: Similar to the tabling method, this approach utilizes a cold marble slab to accelerate the cooling process. After partially melting chocolate, spread it onto the chilled stone and use spatulas or scrapers to mix and fold the chocolate until properly tempered. Once achieved, gather the now-tempered chocolate back into a bowl for further usage.

Moreover, incorporating trendy flavors or sustainable ingredients adds modern twists to traditional recipes, appealing to contemporary tastes and dietary preferences.

Spatial Relationships in ASL:
One essential aspect of ASL grammar is the use of space to convey meaning. Unlike spoken languages where words follow a linear order, ASL signs can occur simultaneously in various locations around the signer's body. This allows for greater flexibility in expressing complex ideas and maintaining cohesion throughout discourse. For example, when describing the act of immigration, a signer might establish two distinct areas representing 'home country' and 'destination,' then produce specific signs within those spaces to indicate actions taking place between them.

Role Shifting in Narratives:
Another critical feature of ASL grammar is role shifting, which enables signers to take on different character perspectives during narration. Instead of explicitly stating who they are referring to, signers physically shift their position, facial expressions, and movements to represent each participant involved in the story. Role shifting creates a vivid and engaging experience for viewers while also providing crucial information about characters and events being discussed. When recounting personal stories involving multiple people, immigrants using ASL may utilize role shifting to share their journey effectively.

Classifiers and Motion in ASL:
Classifiers play a significant role in ASL grammar, allowing signers to depict objects, entities, and even abstract concepts visually. These classifier predicates consist of handshapes, orientations, and movements that correspond to particular characteristics of the referent. For instance, when discussing transportation methods commonly associated with immigration, such as boats or planes, a signer would select appropriate classifiers to show size, shape, and motion accurately. Classifiers enable ASL users to create dynamic descriptions that engage learners and facilitate deeper comprehension.

Non-Manual Markers in ASL:
In addition to manual signs, ASL utilizes non-manual elements, such as facial expressions and body posture, to convey additional layers of meaning. Known as non-manual markers (NMMs), these subtle cues often accompany signed vocabulary to clarify grammatical functions, emotional states, or rhetorical emphasis. For instance, raised eyebrows can signal interrogative sentences, whereas furrowed brows could denote past tense or negation. Understanding the nuanced usage of NMMs is vital for mastering ASL grammar and capturing the intended message fully.
