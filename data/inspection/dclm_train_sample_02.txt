John Strong writes:

As a friend of mine and natural comic is prone to say, we are in violent agreement!.

Jane Jacobs wrote very persuasively 50 years ago about the "quality of life" benefits to mixed uses (i.e. relaxed zoning regimes) and letting cities develop spontaneously. It makes public transport (including privately owned public transport) feasible. In fact, it is the *only* thing that makes it feasible. And more importantly, it makes walking feasible, as the statistics David Owen provides about New Yorkers and their habits make clear. So it seems that a low energy footprint is yet another advantage to keeping city uses mixed and zoning regimes loose.

Many people think government-owned transport is the best way to reduce energy consumption for the same reason people climb Mt. Everest: because the challenge is "there" (the challenge being to persuade a skeptical public of this elitist policy prescription). In other words, their thinking is dialectical. They are so busy fighting the folks they view as unenlightened that they don't pause to examine the facts on the ground.

Mads Lindstrøm writes:

Great podcast. I loved that the guests seemed to look for realistic answers. Many (obviously not all) other environmentalist have a nag for suggesting things that does not seem to be rooted in reality.

Regarding the discussion of what can be done, it feels like I am missing something, as there seems to be something quite straightforward we can do. If you want less of something you tax it. So why not tax pollution more severely? Possibly only in rich countries.

Note that not all joules of energy we use leads to an equal amount of utility. Thus, if we limit our consumption by taxation, the activities with worst utility/energy ratios will cease.

Also, we are becoming more and more efficient with our existing use of energy. Unfortunately, we also find new ways of using our energy with a very bad utility/energy ratio. But it is the energy usage with very bad utility/energy ratio that a tax would remove.

Taxation do not necessarily mean that we must lower are consumption below our current level. Given that many existing activities becomes more and more energy efficient, it follows that a tax increased a sufficiently small bit each year, would lower our growth in living standard, but not below our current standard of living.

It may not be politically easy to increase gas taxes, but it do not seem completely unlikely either. First of all, most western countries already have gas taxes. Second, it would make it possible to lower other taxes, which could be a selling point to voters.
