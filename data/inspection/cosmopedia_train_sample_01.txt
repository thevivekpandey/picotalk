14.1 Introduction

Spectroscopy is an analytical technique that uses the interaction between electromagnetic radiation and matter to identify and quantify chemical species. The fundamental principle behind spectroscopy lies in the fact that different chemicals absorb, reflect, or emit light at specific wavelengths depending on their molecular structure. In this chapter, we will explore how spectroscopic techniques can be applied in environmental monitoring and assessment, specifically focusing on remote sensing applications such as those mentioned in the webpage extract provided. We will also examine the role of spectroscopy in assessing the impact of human activities, such as coal mining, on nearby ecosystems.

14.2 Principles of Spectroscopy

At the core of spectroscopy is the Beer-Lambert Law, which describes the relationship between the intensity of incident light (I), the transmitted light (I0), the concentration of the analyte (c), and the path length (l): I = I0 \* exp(-εlc). This equation indicates that the amount of absorbed light depends on both the concentration of the absorbing molecule and the distance traveled by the light through the sample. By measuring the intensity of the transmitted light and applying the Beer-Lambert Law, researchers can determine the concentration of a given analyte within a sample.

There are several types of spectroscopy, including absorption, emission, fluorescence, and Raman spectroscopy. Each type utilizes unique interactions between electromagnetic radiation and matter to provide distinct information about the sample under investigation. For instance, absorption spectroscopy involves measuring the decrease in the intensity of incident light due to absorption by the sample. Emission spectroscopy, on the other hand, measures the emitted light generated when excited atoms or molecules return to their ground state after being energized. Fluorescence spectroscopy relies on the phenomenon whereby certain molecules absorb energy and then reemit it at longer wavelengths. Lastly, Raman spectroscopy detects shifts in scattered light resulting from the inelastic scattering of photons by vibrating molecules. These various forms of spectroscopy have been widely used in fields such as chemistry, physics, engineering, and environmental science.

14.3 Remote Sensing Applications of Spectroscopy

Remote sensing refers to the acquisition of data without physical contact with the target object. One common application of remote sensing involves satellite imagery analysis, wherein sensors aboard satellites capture reflected sunlight across various spectral bands. Researchers use these multispectral images to analyze vegetation health, soil moisture levels, water quality, and land cover classification, among other parameters.

In the context of environmental monitoring, spectroscopy plays a critical role in estimating variables such as chlorophyll content, leaf area index, and photosynthetic activity. Additionally, hyperspectral imaging—a form of spectroscopy that captures hundreds of narrow contiguous spectral bands—has emerged as a powerful tool for identifying subtle differences in plant stress and disease detection. Furthermore, spectroscopic approaches enable accurate estimation of atmospheric constituents, allowing scientists to track air pollution patterns and climate change impacts.
